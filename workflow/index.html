The Smart Way

How a commercial building contract actually runs, why the process that protects the money never survives contact with a site, and what a system has to do about it.

ScopeTender to final account
ContractJBCC principal building agreement
MarketCommercial building, South Africa
Updated2026-08-24

Every well-run building contractor already has a process. It is written down, it is usually correct, and it is almost never followed, because it runs to several hundred steps and nobody can hold that in their head while standing in a trench. The process is not the problem. Carrying it is.

Reduce a commercial building contract to the parts that actually decide whether the job makes money, and you are left with a small, hard set of numbers.

01

A building contract is a task list with a clock on it

Strip out the detail and a whole project resolves to about 48 steps that somebody has to own on a date. Each one needs the same three fields, and only three.

Every step that costs money when it slips carries the same shape:

Person Responsiblewho owns it
Date - Recurring / Once off target datewhen, and whether it repeats
Status - Incomplete / Completebinary state, no in-between

Three fields, and the third is the interesting one. There is no "in progress" and no percentage complete. A step is done or it is not. That is the right discipline for a phone, and it is the only way a completion bar tells the truth.

They split cleanly in two: what must be true before anybody is on site, and what runs while the work is happening. Different owners, different rhythms, and very different consequences for missing them.

Why it matters

A project is 48 over 48, and each of the 48 has a named owner. That is a progress number a director can trust, because nothing in it is a judgement call.

02

Five contract editions, running at the same time

This is the one that justifies the whole product.

A contractor with eight live projects is running five editions of the same contract at once: March 2005, July 2007, March 2014, May 2018 and Minor Works. Not as history, but concurrently, because every job runs on the edition it was signed under and a building contract lasts years.

The clause numbers move between editions. Worse, the deadlines move. Same event, same company, same site agent, different arithmetic:

After practical completion July 2007 March 2014 May 2018
Practical completion clause241919
Defects liability clause26.021.021.0
PA issues final account within90 wd90 wd60 wd
Contractor objects within45 wd45 wd30 wd
PA inspects or deemed complete7 days5 wd + 5 wd5 wd + 5 wd
Latent defects liability5 years5 years5 years
Clause reference34.1 / 34.426.10 / 26.1126.10 / 26.11

Read the highlighted rows again. A site agent who learned the trade under the 2014 edition has 45 working days burned into his head as the window to object to a final account. On a 2018 contract he has 30. If he uses the number he knows, he loses the right to object on a figure that is usually the largest single number in the whole job.

That is not a training problem you fix with a course. It is a problem you fix by never asking a human to remember the number.

How easy this is to get wrong

The first cut of the app used 90 working days for the final account on a May 2018 job, because 90 is the figure that appears most often. It is the 2007 and 2014 number. The 2018 number is 60. That is the exact mistake the tool exists to prevent, made deliberately and carefully by someone with the contract open, which is the strongest argument there is for never asking a person to recall the figure at all.

04

The seam between the existing systems is where the product lives

South African contractors are not running on paper. They are running on two good products with a gap between them.

Estimating and programming sit in Candy. Accounting sits in BuildSmart. Both are mature, both are entrenched, and neither is going anywhere. Replacing them is not the opportunity, and attempting it is how these projects die.

What neither does is the bit in between: the site agent standing in the rain who has just been handed an instruction, and the contractual clock that started the moment he read it. Candy knows the rate. BuildSmart knows the cost. Nothing knows that a notice is due in seventeen working days.

This is the whole argument for the product. A portfolio dashboard reports things that already happened. The gap is about the thing that is about to stop happening, in front of the person who can still act on it.

Build

Voice and an on-device model

The right split is not "put an LLM in the app". It is: the model handles language, and never handles consequences.

Putting Gemma on the handset is the correct instinct for this market, for three reasons that have nothing to do with it being fashionable.

Signal. A basement, a lift shaft, a site outside Malmesbury. The moment the app needs a round trip to answer, it stops being used, and an app that is used 80% of the time on a contractual deadline is worth close to nothing.

Hands. The site agent is holding a tape, a drawing and a phone. Voice is not a novelty here, it is the only input mode that works while the work is happening. Typing a variation into a form at 16:30 back in the container is how the detail gets lost.

Language. This is the part that does not travel backwards from an overseas product. A foreman describing a defect in Afrikaans or isiXhosa, transcribed and slot-filled on the device, is a genuine moat on a South African site and it travels to every market where the person doing the work does not draft in the contract language.

What that means in practice

  • Model. Gemma 3n at the E2B size is built for this: it runs in roughly 2 to 3 GB of memory on a mid-range Android handset and handles the two jobs we need, which are extraction and phrasing. Slot filling alone would run on something smaller still.
  • Speech. On-device recognition first, with the local model repairing the transcript against a site vocabulary. "P and G", "snag", "subbie", "shutter", grid references like "C4 to D5" and rand amounts spoken as "eighty eight four" are exactly what a general recogniser gets wrong.
  • Read-back is not optional. Voice input without confirmation is how you get a variation submitted at R884 000 instead of R88 400. Every voice-captured number gets repeated back and tapped to confirm. That single rule is what makes voice safe enough to trust on a contract.
  • Offline-first is the real engineering. The model is the easy part. A sync queue that survives a week without signal, resolves conflicts when two people edited the same claim, and never loses a photo, is the part that takes the time. It is also the part that makes the product hard to copy.
  • Battery and heat. Keep the model asleep. It wakes for a capture and goes back down. Anything that runs the model continuously will not survive a real day on site.
Build

Idiot-proof, and a game worth playing

Both requirements point at the same 48 control points. One makes them impossible to get wrong, the other makes them worth doing.

Robust enough for a site

The design rule is that the user should never be able to produce an invalid state, and should never have to remember anything a machine could know.

  • One question per screen, tap by default. Typing is the exception, voice is the shortcut, and the keyboard never appears for anything a machine can compute.
  • No user ever types a date. Every deadline is derived from an event date and the contract edition. This removes the single largest category of error in the whole domain.
  • Gates that actually block. The submit button does not exist until the conditions are true. Already built and it changes the feel of the thing immediately.
  • Evidence is captured, not attached. Photo, geotag, timestamp, locked. If it can be edited afterwards it is worth nothing in a dispute, so it cannot be.
  • Everything is resumable. A dropped call, a flat battery or a two-day gap picks up on the step it left, because the flow is state, not a form.
  • Sunlight and gloves. Large targets, high contrast, no hover, no long press, no gesture anyone has to be taught.
  • Say what happens. "Send to principal agent" and then a confirmation that says it was sent. Never "Submit", never a silent success.

Gamified, in a way that survives contact with a foreman

Points and badges will be laughed off a construction site inside a week. What will not be laughed off is a score people already get paid on. Contractors already rate their site teams once a year and hang increases and bonuses off that rating, usually from memory and a spreadsheet. The game is not an addition to the process. It is something the business already does badly, done properly.

  • The score is rand and days, not points. Money protected, notices served in time, days of extension claimed. Real outcomes from real claims, per site agent, per project.
  • Streaks on the three daily forced items. RFI updated, concrete cast register updated, progress report written against the previous report. These are the three that decay quietly, and the three nobody misses until a claim needs them.
  • The completion bar is 48 over 48. Not a percentage of a guess. Every project carries the same denominator and a named owner against every open item, which makes it social without the software naming and shaming anybody.
  • The guides are the certification. Run a flow with the chatbot three times and it stops holding your hand; the record shows you can run it unsupervised. That is a training programme that costs nothing to deliver and produces an audit trail of competence, which is also useful for the health and safety file.
  • Toolbox talks as short quizzes. Induction and toolbox talks are already control points on the sheet. Two questions on a phone, logged, is better evidence than a signature on a sheet in a container.
  • Reward the early notice, not the volume. Whatever gets scored gets gamed, so score the thing that cannot be gamed usefully: serving the notice inside the window. A contractor who games that metric is a contractor who never misses a claim.
The connection worth pitching

Training, gamification and performance reviews are the same feature. The guide teaches the step, the step is a control point, the control point has an owner, and the owner's record of on-time control points is what the directors already sit down and rate them on once a year. Nobody has to be sold on a new process, because there is no new process.

Next

What I would build in the next cut

  1. Contract edition as a first-class object. Set it once at handover, and every deadline in the app derives from it. This is a day of work and it is the difference between a demo and a product.
  2. The progress report flow. Forced update against the previous report, allowable versus actual, the saving surfaced. It is the piece that makes the monitoring half of the story real.
  3. Voice capture on one flow only. Site instruction to variation order, with read-back confirmation. One flow done properly proves the whole architecture and demos better than six half-flows.
  4. The 48-point project spine. Owner, target date, binary status. Every other feature hangs off this one object, and getting its shape right is worth more than any three features.

Note on the numbers

Clause references, deadlines and edition differences are set out here to show the shape of the problem, not as legal advice, and have not been verified line by line against the published JBCC documents. Any implementation has to do that verification against the executed contract for the specific project, which is exactly the work the system exists to take off a person.
