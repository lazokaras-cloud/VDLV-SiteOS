The Process Ontology

The missing half of the discovery workshop. Parts 1 to 3 establish why, who and what cannot move. This part captures how the work actually runs, in the one shape a system can be built from.

The short version

We do not need a beautiful document. We need the decisions, the numbers and the deadlines. If you have to choose, skip the prose and give us the rules.

Where this sits

Slide 7 gets the shape. This gets the substance.

Walking one project from award to close-out surfaces the steps and the hand-off failures. It does not surface the numbers that decide whether the job made money, because nobody says those out loud unless asked directly.

Why this shape

Software can copy a process. It cannot guess a rule.

Anyone can draw the steps from a workshop. What no outsider can invent is the part that decides whether the job makes money.

Easy to say, low value

"The site agent submits the claim to the QS each month."

True, and worth nothing. Every contractor does this.

Harder to say, this is the product

"The claim must be in by the 25th. Anything late waits a month. Subs get paid 7 days after the certificate is approved, and only once the client has paid, at their rate without our markup."

The second one contains a date, a consequence, a dependency and a money rule. Four things a system can enforce. The first is a sentence.

Rule of thumb

If a step has a number, a deadline or a person who can say no, we need it. If it has none of those, one line is plenty.

Structure

Three passes

A

The operating picture

One page. What gets built, at what size, on which contracts, with which systems.

B

Name the areas

A list, in the order work happens, using whatever the business calls them.

C

Six fields per area

The real work. Steps, decisions, rules, inputs, outputs, dependencies.

Documentation to gather
  • Standard form contract and edition currently in use
  • One priced bill and one baseline programme
  • A sample monthly claim and its certificate
  • Any existing SOPs, checklists or wall charts
Working with Claude

Paste the six fields for one area and ask it to write back the state machine it would build: the states, the transitions, and every deadline it would compute. Where it guesses, you have found a gap in the answers.

Pass A

The operating picture

Context that stops the wrong thing being built. Bullet points are fine.

1What gets built, at what typical value, over how many months?A hospital and a warehouse are different products
2How many jobs run at once, and who carries more than one?Decides whether the portfolio view matters on day one
3Which standard form contracts get signed, and which editions are live right now?Every deadline in the system comes from this answer
4Which roles can approve what, and to what value?Roles, not names
5Where does money leak today, in the honest version?Usually two or three known sore points
Most valuable single answer

The last one. Name the three places you already know money goes missing and those get built first, which means the thing pays for itself before anyone has to trust it.

Pass C

Six fields, every area, every time

Repetition is the point. It makes areas comparable and it makes gaps obvious.

1Steps. What actually happens, in order. Short lines."Receive instruction, price it, notify, submit claim"
2Decision points. Where a human chooses, and the options."Claimable or our cost? Time-related or money only?"
3Rules and thresholds. Numbers, deadlines, percentages, approval limits. The most valuable field here."Notice within 20 working days. Cost plus 10%. Over R50k needs the director."
4Inputs. What must exist before the step starts, and where it lives today."Written instruction, priced bill, daywork sheets, photos"
5Outputs. What the step produces, and who receives it."Priced variation to the PA, updated cost report, diary entry"
6Dependencies. What breaks it. Other steps, other people, other systems."Cannot claim until the QS certifies. Blocks the monthly claim."
Worked example

One area, filled in

This is the useful level of detail. It took about ten minutes to write.

FieldSite instruction to variation
1. StepsPA issues written instruction → site agent logs it → measure actual labour, plant, material → add markup → photograph the work → serve notice → submit priced claim → track the response
2. DecisionsIs the instruction in writing? Did it cost time as well as money? Is the delay claimable or ours? Claim now or with the monthly?
3. RulesNotice within 20 working days of becoming aware. Priced claim within 40. PA must respond within 20 or it is deemed refused. Priced at actual cost plus 10%, never off a bill rate. Verbal instructions are not variations.
4. InputsWritten instruction, priced bill, daywork sheets signed by the foreman, plant hours, delivery notes, photographs, site diary
5. OutputsNotice to the PA, priced variation, updated contract value, diary entries for the 40 and 20 day dates, an extension claim if time was lost
6. DependenciesNeeds the baseline from tender to measure against. Feeds the monthly claim. Blocked if the foreman's sheets are unsigned. Delay events feed the programme update.
Notice what field 3 did

Four numbers and one prohibition. That row alone is enough to build a countdown, a calculation, a validation gate and an automatic escalation. Everything else on the page is context.

Calibration

Vague versus buildable

The same step written two ways. Only one of them can be built.

Too vague

"We review sub-contractor performance regularly and take action where necessary."

Buildable

"More than 5 days behind programme, the site agent serves a written notice. 5 working days to fix it. Still behind, we can terminate and the cost to complete goes to his account. Two notices in one contract and he comes off the approved list."

Too vague

"Payments are made according to the contract terms."

Buildable

"Subs are paid 7 calendar days after the certificate is approved, and only once the client has paid us. Their rate, no markup. Contra charges come off before the remittance goes out."

Watch for these words

"As appropriate", "regularly", "as required", "where necessary". Every one of them has a number hiding behind it, and that number is the answer we came for.

Priority

Everything with a clock on it

If nothing else gets captured, capture this. It is a short list, and it is where the value sits.

CaptureDetail
Every deadlineNotice periods, claim windows, response times, cut-offs. State whether each counts working days or calendar days.
Every thresholdApproval limits, markup percentages, retention, penalty rates, discount terms.
Every "or else"What happens when a deadline is missed. Deemed refused, deemed complete, right lost, penalty applies.
Every approvalWho can say yes, to what value, and what happens when they say nothing at all.
Every recurrenceDaily, weekly, monthly, and the event the clock starts from.
Say where each rule comes from

Contract clause, company policy, or "the way we have always done it". All three are legitimate and all three behave differently: contract rules change with the edition, policy rules change when a director says so, and habits are usually the ones worth questioning.

Scope

What not to bother with

Polish

Bullet points, a voice note, a photograph of a whiteboard. All fine. Nobody is marking the grammar.

Completeness first time

Two areas done properly beats eight done thinly. Start where the money leaks.

The obvious

We know what a building site is. Skip the general description, go to what is specific about how this business does it.

Screen ideas

Give the rule, not the interface. If the rule is right, the screen follows from it.

Consensus

Where two people disagree about the process, record both. The disagreement is a finding.

The tidy version

The workarounds are the point. If it really runs on WhatsApp and a spreadsheet, say so.

Capture

Lowest effort that works

1

Talk it

Voice notes against the six fields, one area at a time. Fastest by a distance, and the tone survives.

2

Table it

A spreadsheet, one row per step, six columns. Better when several people contribute.

3

Show it

Photograph what is already on the wall, then talk through the parts that are not written down.

Who should answer

The person who does the work, not the person who owns the process. The gap between the documented process and the real one is the most valuable thing in this exercise, and only one of those two can see it.

Working with Claude

Send the voice notes straight through. Ask for the six fields back as a table, then read it against what was actually said. Anything it filled in smoothly that nobody mentioned is a gap, not a result.

Return

What comes back

So the effort has something to be measured against.

FromYou get
Rules and thresholdsA deadline engine that computes every date from the contract edition and the event, so nobody has to remember which number applies to which job
Steps and decisionsThe guided flows: one question per screen, with the gates that stop an invalid submission
Inputs and outputsThe data model, and the integration points into the estimating and accounting systems already in use
DependenciesThe escalation logic, and the portfolio view that shows which clock runs out first across every job
Timing

One area, properly captured, is a working prototype in days rather than months. That prototype is the thing to argue about, not the document.

In one line

Give us the rules.
We build the rest.

Steps and screens are the easy half. The numbers, the deadlines and the consequences are what turn a decent interface into something that protects money, and they exist nowhere except inside the business.
